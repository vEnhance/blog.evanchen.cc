## My guest essay on math competitions

Earlier this year I wrote a guest post for an external Substack, called
[What's Happening to the World of Math Olympiads?][ep].
This is an essay about what math competitions are for intended for
people who might not know much about them (i.e., not my usual audience).
At a high level, the main things I tried to explain to the public are:

- The point of math olympiad problems is not just to be difficult,
  but more importantly, to be instructive and enjoyable to work on.
  The design of good olympiad problems is an artisan process.
  We are really, really proud of the problems we publish
  and are delighted when students are excited about them.[^classroom]
- By extension, the point of math contests is to develop interest and skill,
  not merely to measure talent.
- Math contests are _one_ route to good math experiences (not the only one).
  They may not be for everyone; but their ability to scale cheaply
  makes them historically an important player in the ecosystem,
  just by number of students reached.
- … Except AMC participation is down like 4x from the 1980s.
  In part because it is now considered a niché activity
  and is often inaccessible to outsiders.

### Footnote 8: Proofs are for everyone

The 8th footnote is the one that is really meant for this blog audience:

> In general, as you advance to later rounds of math competitions, the amount of
> assumed background knowledge increases, the time limits change from minutes to
> hours, and real proofs are demanded (rather than just a numerical answer).
> For a problem-setter, that gives you way more power to design a learning experience.
> So it's actually the latest stages of the competition (like the IMO)
> which have the best problems showcased.
> Whereas on the other extreme, the entry-level AMC has a
> large speed component that is [often rightly criticized][speed],
> and its format as a multiple-choice exam is a major weakness in its design.
> This means the best pedagogical value is disproportionately concentrated on
> the most advanced stages of the competition series.

I've started thinking that as far as serving students go,
even for newcomers I've been moving further away
from fast numerical-answer exams towards slower long-form exams.
(As long as the grading bottleneck can be handled.)
I just feel like they are much better solving and learning experiences.
Plus the student feels respected by someone actually reading their work.
Actually, I think many non-US countries have proofs much earlier on:
[see my comment on that Substack for examples][substack-comment].
In slogan form: "proofs are for everyone".
