Over the last 10 years, I have read every single problem set submission to OTIS.
I think this is almost 20,000 submissions to date.[^count]

[^count]:
    For the OTIS students looking at the ID numbers on problem set submissions:
    those go up to about 15k now, but there were also submissions
    before OTIS-WEB had a dedicated `PSet` model.

So here's something that I think will surprise you:
the submissions don't look anything remotely alike.
The variation in work quality is _staggering_.
Since I'm not going to show any samples for privacy reasons,
I'm not going to mince words about this either:

- On one extreme, some submissions are breathtakingly well-polished.
  They are neatly typeset with full-color diagrams and read well.
  They're better than what you would see in most published textbooks.
- On the other extreme, some submissions are incomprehensible.
  They remind me of xkcd [1513][1513], [1695][1695], [1833][1833];
  I couldn't write that badly if I tried.
  (This last sentence is important because without it,
  you might think some people don't want to spend time
  polishing their solutions so they can solve more problems.
  But the worst submissions are so poor that I don't believe this[^time].)

[^time]:
    It works the other way too.
    If you always do a good job, then doing a good job becomes faster.

[1513]: https://xkcd.com/1513/
[1695]: https://xkcd.com/1695/
[1833]: https://xkcd.com/1833/

[Richard Rusczyk noticed this too 16 years ago](https://artofproblemsolving.com/community/c864h981002),
and now I understand what he means.

> Even 6 years later, I'm amazed at the huge variance in the quality of work
> that students submit on the Challenge Sets in our classes. Part of that, of
> course, is the fact that we don't give grades in our classes. But what I find
> amusing is that the typical classroom would see all of these students as
> exactly the same -- they're pretty much all students who ace everything in
> their regular schools. So, they all look the same in their classrooms. They
> look very different to me. That's important, I think.

I always think to myself that if I was trying to hire people for a team[^hiring],
I would care way more about this than just an exam score.
**Who has a habit of doing things well?**
This is one of the reasons I am so consistently disappointed by the
competition culture that only getting 7 points matters.[^protest]

[^hiring]:
    I do feel a bit bad using hiring as an example
    because I also don't think people's lives should revolve around being
    The Perfect Employee.

[^protest]:
    The most inflammatory cases are the ones where people loudly complain they
    didn't get points on a problem they (thought they) solved.
    Normally, if multiple graders didn't give you points on a problem because
    _they legitimately couldn't follow what you wrote_,
    I would hope that students respond like
    "I should try to improve my writing so I can get full marks next year",
    rather than "I am being screwed over by an arrogant
    power-tripping organization that can't face repercussions".
    The latter kind of response is far more common now than 10 years ago.

This goes beyond just writing ability to me,
but a more general sense of **craftsmanship**.
From a hiring perspective, the word I'd use instead is **trust**:
if I delegate a task to someone, do I expect to be happy with the output?

Maybe it sounds frivolous and ridiculous, but I'll say it anyway:
I would be worried hiring someone who types $\sin(\theta)$ as $sin(theta)$.
